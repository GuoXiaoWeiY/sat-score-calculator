A score calculator looks backward: it turns answers you have already gotten right into a scaled score for a test you have taken. This SAT score predictor looks forward instead. It starts from where you are now — a recent PSAT or full-length practice-test score — and projects where you could realistically land on test day given the study time you put in between now and then. That makes it a planning tool: use it to decide whether your goal is on track, and how much time you need to get there.

How this SAT score predictor works

The model is deliberately simple and transparent, so you can understand and sanity-check the number. It has three steps:

  1. Normalize your starting score to the 400–1600 SAT scale. If you entered a SAT practice-test score, it is used as-is. If you entered a PSAT score (320–1520), it is scaled up by roughly 1.05 to a SAT equivalent, because the PSAT runs on a slightly shorter scale.
  2. Estimate total study hours as weeks remaining multiplied by hours per week. This is the single biggest lever you control.
  3. Apply a point-gain curve with diminishing returns. Each hour is worth more when you are starting from a lower score, and the gain flattens as you approach the top of the scale. The result is added to your starting score and shown with a typical ±40-point uncertainty band.

Why the diminishing-returns curve? Scoring is roughly linear through the middle of the range, so early hours buy the most points. Above about 1400, each additional 100 points requires substantially more work because you are already answering most questions correctly and the remaining errors are hard, high-value ones. Any predictor that promises the same point gain per hour at 900 and at 1500 is not being honest.

PSAT to SAT score prediction

The PSAT/NMSQT and PSAT 10 are scored on a 320–1520 scale and are the earliest reliable signal of where you stand. For planning, a rough SAT equivalent is about 1.05 times your PSAT total. The table below gives a sense of the mapping before study time is factored in.

Treat this as directional. A PSAT taken in October of sophomore or junior year understates what you can do a year later, after more coursework and test familiarity. The predictor deliberately leaves room for that growth through the study-time input rather than inflating the starting number.

Using a practice-test score to predict test day

Your starting score is the foundation of any prediction, so it is worth getting a good one. A few rules make the input more meaningful:

  • Prefer a full-length Bluebook test. The Digital SAT is module-adaptive; a paper or prep-book test cannot reproduce the routing that shapes your real scaled score.
  • Use your most recent attempt. If you have several dated scores, average your last two so one bad day does not drag the projection down.
  • Take it under real conditions. Timed, quiet, one sitting. An untimed score at home systematically overstates what you will produce on test day.
  • Track the trend, not one number. A rising sequence of three scores is far more predictive than any single result.

Once you have a projected score, compare it to the benchmark for the colleges on your list rather than to a national average. If your projection sits below a school's middle 50%, the gap tells you exactly how many more hours you need — divide the point gap by your current gain rate to get a rough time estimate.

What the prediction cannot tell you

No tool can account for everything. Test-day anxiety, illness, a difficult form, or an unusually strong week of review can all move your real score outside the predicted band. The Digital SAT also uses question equating, which means the same raw performance can convert to slightly different scaled scores across test forms. And motivation is not constant — planning eight hours a week and actually completing eight hours a week are different things.

For all those reasons, use this predictor the way you would use a weather forecast: as a reason to prepare, not a guarantee of the outcome. Recalculate it after every full-length practice test. As your actual scores replace projected ones, the range narrows and the forecast gets more useful.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is an SAT score predictor?

Treat the output as a planning range, not a promise. This predictor uses a transparent model based on your starting score and total study hours, with a typical uncertainty of about ±40 points. Real results depend on how you study, test-day conditions, and the equating of your particular form. Only College Board can issue an official score.

Can you predict an SAT score from a PSAT score?

Yes, approximately. The PSAT/NMSQT is scored on a 320–1520 scale and correlates closely with the SAT. This tool converts your PSAT total to a rough SAT equivalent (about a 1.05× scale adjustment) before applying the study-time projection. Most students score somewhat higher on the SAT after a year of additional schooling.

How many SAT points can I gain in a few months?

It depends on your starting point and hours. A common range is 40–90 points for 20–30 hours of focused prep, 80–150 points for 50–90 hours, and 150–220 points for 100+ hours. Gains are usually larger from a lower starting score and noticeably smaller above 1400.

What is the difference between a predictor and a score calculator?

A score calculator converts answers you have already gotten right into a scaled score for a test you have taken. A score predictor projects the score you could reach on a future test from your current level plus planned study time. Use the calculator after a practice test, and this predictor when planning your prep.

Does the Digital SAT predict differently than the old SAT?

The score scale is the same 400–1600, but the Digital SAT is module-adaptive and shorter. Use a full-length Bluebook practice test as your starting score when possible; its adaptive routing makes it a better predictor than paper tests or unofficial prep-book tests.

Which practice-test score should I enter?

Use your most recent full-length practice test under timed conditions, ideally in Bluebook. If you have several dated attempts, enter an average of your last two so a single bad day does not skew the projection.
