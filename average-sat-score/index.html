Average SAT Score in 2026

The national average total score was 1029 for the class of 2025 — 521 in Reading & Writing and 508 in Math. Here are the published numbers year by year, what the median is, and why the average is not the number you should be aiming at.

Updated November 2026 · Figures from College Board Total Group reports, class of 2025 is the most recent complete cohort

Quick answer: the average SAT total is 1029 (Reading & Writing 521, Math 508). The median is slightly lower, near 1010. Above 1100 puts you past roughly 62% of test takers; above 1200 puts you in the top quarter.

College Board publishes average scores for each graduating class in its Total Group reports. Each year's class is a different group of students — including a growing share who test during school days under state contracts — so the averages reflect who took the test, not whether the test got easier.

The interesting context is not the score column but participation. The class of 2025 cohort was about 2.0 million students — roughly a third larger than the pandemic-era class of 2021. When a test population grows that fast in four years, the mean moves because the population changed. This is a composition effect, not a difficulty effect.

Why averages moved after 2021

Two forces pushed in opposite directions, which is why the trend looks like a dip and a slow recovery rather than a straight line.

Downward pressure: universal in-school testing. A growing number of states and districts now administer the SAT to an entire grade during a school day, at no cost. That adds students who would never have signed up voluntarily — and by definition, students who are not self-selected for test-taking skill. Every cohort that expands this way drags the mean toward the middle and below.

Upward pressure: test-optional self-selection. At the same time, many colleges stopped requiring scores, so some weaker-scoring students chose not to test at all — which removes low scores from the pool and pushes the mean up.

The drop from 1060 in 2021 to 1024 in 2024 reflects the first force winning. The partial recovery to 1029 in 2025 reflects the second. Neither movement tells you anything about whether the questions got harder: the Digital SAT's scaled scores are equated across forms precisely so that year-to-year difficulty differences do not show up in the distribution.

Don't read the trend as a signal about your chances. A falling national mean does not make your 1200 worth more in admissions. Colleges compare you to their own applicant pool, and that pool's average has been rising at selective schools even while the national average fell.

Mean vs median: why 1029 is not the middle

The 1029 figure is a mean — add up every score and divide. The median, the score with half of test takers above and half below, sits near 1010, at about the 50th percentile. The two differ because the distribution is not symmetric: there is a long, thin lower tail (scores in the 400s and 500s) and a compressed upper tail, since the scale stops at 1600.

That single fact explains several things that confuse people:

  • A score of 1029 is not the 50th percentile — it is the 48th. Half of test takers score below roughly 1010, not below 1029.
  • The mean is pulled up by the upper tail, so "average" sounds better than it feels. Being exactly at the mean means you are very slightly below the middle of the pack.
  • Because the middle is crowded, small score moves change percentiles quickly there. From 1000 to 1100 is a 15-point percentile jump; from 1400 to 1500 it is 5.

Why averages differ so much by state

State-level averages range from roughly the mid-900s to above 1200, and the spread has almost nothing to do with school quality. It tracks participation rate instead.

  • Near-universal testing states — where everyone in a grade takes the SAT on a school day — post means in the 940–1050 band. Almost no one is missing from the sample, so the mean reflects the whole grade.
  • Self-selected states — where the SAT is optional and competes with the ACT — post means above 1100, sometimes well above. Only students who expect to apply to selective colleges bother to test, so the sample is filtered.
  • Mixed states fall in between, and their year-to-year swings are often just a policy change in one district.

If you see a state average that looks impressive or alarming, check the participation rate before drawing any conclusion. A state where 100% of students test and a state where 30% test are not measuring the same thing.

What the average means for you

Three uses, in order of how much they should influence your decisions:

  1. As a floor check. Below 1029 you are in the bottom half nationally, which is fine for open-admission and many regional colleges but below range at selective schools. There is also nothing wrong with it — this band has the most room to improve quickly, typically 100–200 points in a few months.
  2. As a progress baseline. Tracking your own practice scores against 1029 gives you a stable, non-emotional reference point. It is a fixed number, unlike "good enough", which moves every time you read another college's website.
  3. As a scholarship reference. Many automatic merit tiers at public universities start at 1200–1350, which is 170–320 points above the national mean. That is the number with money attached.

What the average should not be used for is setting an admissions target. Selective universities report middle-50% ranges of about 1350–1500, which is 320 to 470 points above the national mean. If your goal is a selective school, the national average is roughly irrelevant — go read that school's own range and use our guide to what counts as a good SAT score to turn it into a target.

One more caution: the average describes test takers, not all high school students and not the students at any particular college. A 1029 is average for the country and below average at every selective school in it, and both statements are simultaneously true.

Tracking your own average

Take a full-length practice test every three to four weeks, estimate each result with the calculator, and chart the trend rather than reacting to any single sitting. Three rules make the number meaningful:

  • Same conditions every time — no phone, no extra time, one continuous sitting. A score taken with music on is not a data point.
  • Use Bluebook for official practice tests, since only the adaptive version reproduces the module routing that affects your real scaled score. Our College Board scoring guide explains why a paper practice test converts differently.
  • Judge the three-test moving average, not the latest number. A single test can swing 40–60 points from fatigue, and that noise will make you change your plan for no reason.

If the moving average has been flat for three tests, the plan is the problem, not the effort. Change the question types you drill before you change the number of hours you put in.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average SAT score in 2026?

For the class of 2025, the most recent full cohort, the mean total was 1029 — 521 in Reading & Writing and 508 in Math. Averages have moved between 1024 and 1060 since 2019, so 1029 is the number to use as a reference point.

Is 1050 above average on the SAT?

Yes, slightly. A 1050 is about the 55th percentile, roughly 20 points above the 1029 mean. It is average for the country but still below the middle-50% range at most four-year colleges.

Why is the average lower than it used to be?

More students now take the SAT through universal in-school state testing, which expands the test-taking population to include students who would not have tested voluntarily. The mean reflects who takes the test, not a change in test difficulty.

What is the median SAT score?

The 50th percentile sits near 1010, slightly below the 1029 mean. The distribution has a long lower tail, which pulls the average down more than it moves the middle value.

Is the average the same in every state?

No. State averages run from roughly the mid-900s to above 1200, and the gap tracks participation rate more than school quality. States where nearly everyone tests post lower averages.

How far above average do I need to be for a selective college?

Selective universities report middle-50% ranges of roughly 1350 to 1500, which is 320 to 470 points above the national mean. The national average is a sanity check, not an admissions benchmark.
