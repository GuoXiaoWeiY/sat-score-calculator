Which percentile table should you read?

This is the single most confusing thing about SAT percentiles, and almost every other chart online quietly picks one column without saying which. College Board publishes two, and they answer two different questions.

  • User group percentile — how you rank among students who actually took the SAT and graduated in the last three school years. This is the comparison colleges and most score reports use, because your competition for admission is people who took the test.
  • Nationally representative percentile — how you rank against a weighted sample of all U.S. 11th and 12th graders, including the ones who never sit for the SAT. Because the sample includes non-test-takers, who would mostly score low, every high score looks better here.

That is why a 1200 is the 75th percentile in one column and the 81st in the other. Neither number is wrong. The gap widens as scores rise, because the nationally representative sample adds a large mass of students at the bottom of the scale.

Rule of thumb: use the user group column when you are thinking about admissions, and the nationally representative column only when you want to know how you compare with your entire high school class. The percentile printed on your own score report is a third variant — the "all testers" percentile, which has no public lookup table.

The gap between the two curves is the students who never take the SAT. It is widest above 1200, which is exactly the range where families tend to compare numbers.

Two things fall out of this table. First, roughly 58% of test takers land between 800 and 1190 — the middle is crowded. Second, only 7% break 1400, which is why the curve flattens so hard at the top: there simply are not many people left to pass. If you are at 1450 and want the 99th percentile, you are not competing with the average student, you are competing with the top 4%.

Engineering programs read the Math number, humanities programs read the Reading & Writing number, and some scholarships read the total only. Why 100 points is worth more at 1000 than at 1500

Percentile gains are not linear, and the asymmetry is the most useful fact on this page for planning.

  • 900 → 1000: 32nd to 47th percentile, a 15-point jump for 100 points of score.
  • 1000 → 1100: 47th to 62nd, another 15 points for the same 100.
  • 1200 → 1300: 75th to 86th, an 11-point jump.
  • 1400 → 1500: 93rd to 98th, 5 points.
  • 1500 → 1600: 98th to 99+, maybe 2 points on the printed scale.

The reason is mechanical: each percentile represents a fixed number of people, and the number of people sitting in the 1400s is small. Passing the last few hundred is slow work because there is almost nobody left to pass. This is why a student at 1050 can realistically gain 150 points in three months while a student at 1480 might gain 40 in the same time and call it a win.

It also means the emotional experience of prep changes as you climb. Below 1200, almost every week of focused work moves the percentile number. Above 1450, the number can sit still for a month while your raw score is genuinely improving. Judge progress by section raw scores and by the number of question types you have actually fixed, not by percentile alone.

Where the leverage is: a 30 to 40 point gain between 1000 and 1100 is worth about 5 percentile points, the same as a 100-point gain near 1450. If your goal is "be above most test takers", the cheapest path is almost always to leave the middle of the curve, not to chase the ceiling.

Turning a percentile into a target score

Percentiles are national. Admission is local. The number that actually matters is the middle-50% range of admitted students at the schools on your list — the 25th to 75th percentile of the people that school let in. Working backwards from that table:

  1. Find the bottom edge of the middle 50% at each school you would realistically attend. That is your floor, not your goal.
  2. Convert it to a percentile using the chart above. A 1350 floor means you need to be around the 90th percentile.
  3. Take a full practice test and convert your raw score with the free score calculator, then look up where you landed.
  4. Set the target at the 75th percentile of admitted students, not the 25th. Being inside the range makes your score neutral; being above it makes it a strength.
  5. Re-check monthly. Percentile movement is coarser and easier to feel than raw point movement, but use section scores to steer your prep.

If the gap between your current percentile and your target is more than 20 percentile points, plan on a full retake cycle rather than a quick bump. Four things percentiles do not tell you

1. Percentile is not percent correct

A 75th percentile does not mean you answered 75% of questions correctly. Because the test is equated across dates, the same percent-correct can produce different scaled scores on different test forms. Percentile is a rank against people; percent correct is a property of your answer sheet.

2. It is not your chance of admission

The 75th percentile of all test takers is a very different group from the 25th percentile of admits at a highly selective college. At the top ~30 schools, a 99th percentile national score can sit below the bottom of the admitted range. Percentile tells you where you stand nationally, not whether a particular school will take you.

3. It is not comparable to the old 2400 scale

Scores from before spring 2016 used a different scale, different content, and a penalized-guessing rule. Old percentile tables circulating online are from that era and will mislead you if you read them against a current score.

4. It is a snapshot, not a fixed label

Percentiles are recomputed yearly from the most recent graduating class. A score that was the 90th percentile two years ago might read as the 89th or 91st today. The table on this page is refreshed each admissions cycle, and College Board's official figures live on its Understanding Scores page and research percentile tables.

Frequently asked questions

What percentile is a 1500 SAT score?

About the 98th percentile of SAT test takers, and the 99th percentile of the nationally representative sample. Either way you are in roughly the top 2% of the college-bound group — inside the range at nearly every selective college, though still below the middle of the range at MIT and Harvard.

What percentile is a 1200 SAT score?

About the 75th percentile of test takers (the 81st percentile of all U.S. 11th and 12th graders). That is comfortably above the 1029 national average and competitive at most four-year colleges outside the highly selective tier.

Is the percentile the same for each section?

No. Reading and Writing and Math are scaled and distributed separately, so a 600 in Math sits around the 77th percentile while a 600 in Reading & Writing sits around the 74th. The gap is small in the middle of the scale and grows near the extremes.

Why do two different percentile tables exist?

Because "how do I compare with people who took the test" and "how do I compare with my whole grade" are different questions. The user group table answers the first; the nationally representative table answers the second by including students who never take the SAT. Colleges and score reports use the user group.

Does a high percentile guarantee admission?

No. Percentiles compare you with all test takers nationally, while admission compares you with that school's applicant pool. At the most selective colleges, most applicants are already in the top few percent, so the score stops being a differentiator and the rest of the application decides.

Is percentile the same as percent correct?

No. Percentile is a rank against other people; percent correct is the share of questions you got right. Because the test is equated, the same percent correct can map to different scaled scores on different test dates, so only the scaled score has a percentile.

How many points do I need to move up one percentile?

Near 1000, roughly 25 to 30 points moves you about 5 percentile points. Above 1450 you may need 60 to 100 points for the same gain, because very few test takers sit in that part of the curve. The higher your score, the more expensive each percentile becomes.

Can I compare my percentile with an old SAT score?

Not directly. Scores from before spring 2016 used a 2400 scale with a different content mix and a wrong-answer penalty, so percentiles from that era do not translate to today's 400–1600 scale. If you have an old score, use it only as a rough readiness signal.
