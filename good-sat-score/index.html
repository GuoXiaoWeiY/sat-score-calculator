What Is a Good SAT Score in 2026?

There is no single cutoff. A good score is one that sits at or above the middle of the range at the colleges you actually want — and that number changes completely between a regional state school and MIT. Here are the benchmarks you can plan against.

Updated November 2026 · Benchmarks from College Board percentile tables, Common Data Set ranges and the 2025 SAT Suite Annual Report

Quick answer: the national average is 1029, so 1030+ is above average. 1200 is around the 75th percentile and works at most four-year colleges. 1350 (90th percentile) is competitive at selective public universities. 1500 (98th percentile) is what top-20 colleges expect.

Three different yardsticks for "good"

The SAT is scored from 400 to 1600 by adding two 200–800 section scores, Reading & Writing and Math. Because almost every question on the internet defines "good" against a different comparison group, the same 1250 can be described as excellent, average, or weak depending on who wrote the sentence. There are only three comparisons that matter:

  • Against all test takers. This is the percentile. It answers "how do I rank nationally" and it is useful for scholarships and for a sanity check. A 1250 is around the 82nd percentile.
  • Against your own high school. Your counselor and your classmates' scores shape how your transcript reads locally, and some state scholarship programs are indexed to local rankings rather than national ones.
  • Against a college's admitted class. This is the only comparison that changes an admissions decision. It is published as a middle-50% range — the 25th to 75th percentile of students the school admitted.

Practical rule: benchmarks by college type tell you whether you are in the right neighborhood; the individual school's middle-50% range tells you whether to submit. If a page gives you one number with no comparison group attached, it is not telling you anything you can act on. Our full percentile chart handles the first comparison in ten-point steps.

What each score band means

1500–1600: top 2%

In range at every college in the country. At this level the score stops being a differentiator — admissions readers are comparing essays and coursework, not scaled scores. Diminishing returns for prep are steep here: 50 points can cost months and move you two percentile points.

1400–1490: top 7%

Strong enough for selective private universities and for meaningful merit aid at many public schools. This is the band where "reach" schools become plausible and where a well-rounded application does the rest of the work.

1300–1390: top 15%

Competitive at most selective public universities and comfortably above average everywhere else. A common target for students aiming at a flagship in-state school with a specific major in mind, since some majors (engineering, business) run 50–80 points above the school's overall range.

1200–1290: top quarter

Above roughly three-quarters of test takers and workable at most four-year colleges. This is also where many automatic merit scholarship tiers begin at public universities, so a 1200 is often the first number with a cash value attached.

1030–1190: average to above average

Around the national mean. Not a weak score — half the country is below it — but below the range at selective schools. This band also holds the most room for improvement: gains of 100–150 points in a few months are routine here.

Below 1030: below average, high leverage

Still fine for open-admission colleges and community college transfer paths. It is also the band where prep pays off fastest, because almost every missed question here is a content or pacing gap rather than a hard reasoning problem.

The only official thresholds College Board publishes

College Board does not publish an admissions cutoff, and any site quoting one is inventing it. What it does publish are College and Career Readiness Benchmarks: 480 on Reading & Writing and 530 on Math. College Board describes these as scores that predict a 75% likelihood of earning a C or higher in a related first-semester, credit-bearing college course.

Class of 2025 outcomeShare of test takers

So a 480 + 530 = 1010 is roughly the dividing line between "ready for college-level work" and "not yet", and only about two in five test takers clear both. That is a much more honest floor than any "you need 1200" claim, and it explains why 1010 shows up near the 50th percentile in the full chart. These benchmarks are not admissions cutoffs; no college uses them that way.

Good section scores

Colleges see your two 200–800 section scores, not just the total. A balanced profile reads better than a lopsided one for most programs, and the balance point is not 400/400 — it moves with your intended major.

  • Engineering, computer science, physics: Math 700+ is the effective floor at selective schools; a 650 Math with a 750 Reading will raise questions for a STEM applicant.
  • Business and economics: usually reads the total, but Math above 650 helps, since the quantitative load in the first two years is real.
  • Humanities and social sciences: Reading & Writing 700+ carries weight, and a lower Math score is rarely fatal.
  • Undecided: keep both sections within about 60 points of each other. Balance preserves optionality, which is exactly what an undecided applicant needs.

Section percentiles are not interchangeable — a 600 in Math sits around the 77th percentile while a 600 in Reading & Writing sits around the 74th. Our SAT percentile tables list both columns side by side if you want to check a specific section score.

How to set your personal target

  1. Pick 3–5 colleges you would actually attend — not a dream list, a real list including at least one likely admit.
  2. Find each school's middle-50% range in its Common Data Set (section C) or on the admissions site. Prefer the Common Data Set; marketing pages round up.
  3. Set your floor at the highest bottom edge of those ranges. That is the number below which your score becomes a liability rather than a neutral fact.
  4. Set your goal at the 75th percentile of admitted students at your top realistic choice. Being above the top of the range is what makes a score an asset instead of a checkmark.
  5. Take a full-length practice test and convert your raw score with the free score calculator to see where you stand against that goal.
  6. Track section scores monthly, not the total. A flat total can hide a 40-point Math gain offset by a bad reading day, and section data is what tells you where to spend the next month.
Check the major, not just the school. At many public universities, engineering and business admit by major with ranges 50–100 points above the school-wide middle 50%. A score that is safely in range for the university can be below range for the program you actually want.

If your score is below the range

Three honest options, in order of how often they are the right call:

1. Retake with a targeted plan. Below 1200 the gains are real and repeatable: identify the two or three question types you miss most, drill those specifically, and retake. Students in this band commonly move 100–200 points across two to three months of structured work. Our average score page shows what a normal improvement curve looks like.

2. Go test-optional. Many schools still allow applicants to withhold scores, and a score well below the middle 50% generally hurts more than an absent score. The catch: a growing number of public systems — including several state flagships — have reinstated score requirements, so verify the policy for the specific campus and the specific admission year, not the policy you read about last year.

3. Change the list. If your score is solidly in range at five schools you would be happy to attend, the honest move is to apply there rather than to spend a year chasing a number for a school you are lukewarm about. This is the option applicants consider least and should consider more.

Frequently asked questions

Is 1200 a good SAT score?

Yes for most public universities and many private colleges. A 1200 is about the 75th percentile nationally, roughly 170 points above the 1029 average, and inside the middle-50% range at most non-selective state schools. It is below the typical admit range at top-30 colleges.

Is 1400 a good SAT score in 2026?

Yes. A 1400 is about the 93rd percentile — top 7% of test takers. It sits inside the middle-50% range at selective universities such as Michigan and Cornell, and below the range at MIT and Harvard.

What score do I need to be above average?

The mean total for the class of 2025 was 1029, so 1030 and up is above average. The 50th percentile sits near 1010, which means about half of all test takers score below that line.

Is a 1000 a bad SAT score?

It is average, not bad. A 1000 is around the 47th percentile and close to the national mean. It works at open-admission and many regional colleges, and it is below range at selective schools. It is also a good starting point for a real improvement plan.

Do colleges look at section scores or just the total?

Both. Colleges see your 200–800 section scores alongside the 400–1600 total. STEM programs often check Math specifically and humanities programs read Reading & Writing closely, so a lopsided profile can work against you even when the total looks fine.

Do I need a good score if my college is test-optional?

Only if the score helps. At test-optional schools a score inside or above the middle-50% range strengthens an application; a score well below the range is usually better left out. Check the current policy for each campus — several public systems have gone back to requiring scores.

What SAT score qualifies for merit scholarships?

There is no national cutoff. Many public universities publish automatic merit tiers that begin somewhere between 1200 and 1350, with larger awards above 1400, sometimes paired with a GPA requirement. Private scholarships vary widely, so check the institution's own chart.

How much can I realistically improve my score?

Students starting below 1200 commonly gain 100 to 200 points over two to three months of structured practice. Above 1450 gains are much smaller — typically 30 to 60 points — because the remaining questions are harder and the percentile curve is nearly flat there.
